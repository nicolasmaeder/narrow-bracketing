# Decisions, objectives and information

The paper's three tests are:

- **Lucas critique.** No decision rule is fitted to past policy. Each decision below solves a stated objective using:
  - the decision-maker's own state, including its beliefs;
  - beliefs updated from its own observations or from published statistics;
  - prices posted this quarter or observed last quarter.
- **State-wise computation.** Each quarter is computed once, in a fixed order, as a function of the state. No market is solved for a fixed point within the quarter.
- **Werner accounting.** Loans create deposits and every payment moves money between bank accounts. Bank reserves stay at zero (`check_principles.py`).

Policy rules (the central bank's interest-rate rule and the government's tax rule) are the policy being varied, so they are primitives by design.

## Institutions taken as given (with evidence)

- **Pay review timing:** once a year (Grigsby, Hurst and Yildirmaz).
- **Matching function:** contacts depend on openings per unemployed (Petrongolo and Pissarides).
- **Production:** takes a quarter. Each firm's technology is CES in capital and labor with elasticity 0.5 (Oberfield and Raval 2021), given its technique.
- **Embodiment:** a technique is built into the machines that carry it (Solow 1960; Greenwood, Hercowitz and Krusell 1997; Jones and Liu 2024). The firm's installed technique moves toward the one it now buys at the rate new capital replaces old (capital-weighted geometric average of vintages). In the task framework of Jones and Liu (2024), a firm CES in capital and labor with elasticity 0.5 is exactly what a continuum of tasks with automated share β and capital quality Z gives (capital-augmenting term (Z/β)², labor-augmenting term (1−β)⁻²), so a technique here is an (automation, quality) pair. The investment decision still values new capital at the installed technique (a known approximation).
- **Ideas:** pairs of capital and labor productivity with independent Pareto tails, public, arriving at a rate that grows at a fixed rate (research effort; Jones 2005; Kortum 1997). No aggregate production function is assumed; its long-run form and the direction of technical change are outcomes.

Pay review timing is fixed. Price timing is state-dependent: whether to change price is itself a decision (menu cost), so the frequency of price changes responds to policy.

## Open: variable utilization (Nov 2026)

Diagnosis of why the economy breaks with utilization on (u about 20%):
- Not a customer price war. An option where firms learn how much share their relative price actually wins (`share_learn`, discounted least squares against the logit prediction, prior at 1) gives a learned slope of about 1 in the baseline and does not help with utilization on.
- The demand elasticity eps = 7.7 was calibrated under a steep short-run marginal cost. With flat marginal cost the markup over variable cost alone must cover capital, which needs eps of about 4.5.
- The main amplifier is the fund's valuation: firms are valued at one year of trailing earnings (g_earn = 0.25). With lower margins, earnings and household wealth fall 98% within 30 quarters, consumption falls 45%, and the policy rate sits at zero.
- With a ten-year memory (g_earn = 0.025, the horizon of Shiller's CAPE), the baseline unemployment s.d. falls from 2–3 points to 0.5 and the 20% asset bust raises unemployment only 2 points. With utilization on, u is still about 10% with capacity nearly full: capacity is planned on normal sales, and sales are capped by capacity, so capacity can stay too small.
- Default changed to the ten-year memory (g_earn = 0.025).
- Two further options tried with utilization on, both off by default because they did not help. `cap_unc` plans capacity for orders at the price the firm would charge without a capacity limit; it overbuilds capital and lowers profits, and u rises to about 30%. `clv_lr` values customers at the margin over long-run unit cost; it changes little. A ZLB-off diagnostic (`zlb`) shows the policy-rate floor is not the problem. The remaining mechanism: after the switch, the real wage rises about 30% with no productivity gain (markups over short-run cost fall). Labor-saving techniques are then adopted in a wave (labor productivity +60% in 25 years), and labor capacity falls to about 75% of the labor force.
- **Found and fixed (default):** hiring valued extra output along the long-run demand elasticity (about 8), while the price and stock decision used the short-run one (about 4.6). With flat marginal cost every firm wanted to hire up to capacity, and together they bid wages up: that was the real-wage drift. Hiring now sells extra output along the same demand belief as pricing (`hire_long = False`). Baseline (4 seeds): u 2.6-2.7% (s.d. 0.5-0.7), inflation 1.4-1.6%, price over marginal cost 1.52, wage share 0.42-0.44, K/Y 1.3. With utilization on (eps 6): u 5-6% (s.d. 3-4), utilization 0.93-0.94 against 0.8 planned. Capital stays about 15% below the putty-clay target because the convex adjustment cost (phi_K = 2) is weighed against one quarter's gain rather than the capital's life. Spreading it over the life (`k_amort`) with phi_K 20-35 makes investment self-driven (accelerator) volatility, so it is off.
- 20% asset bust with the new defaults: unemployment +1.5 points at the peak, the price level -13% (still too much deflation), output about -1%.

## Why the price level fell too much in the asset bust (Nov 23)

`price_decomp.py` splits firms' price changes in the bust (relative to the no-shock path, common random numbers). In the first year the fall comes from the demand belief (published market size drops about 5-8%). After that prices follow unit cost, which follows posted pay: pay ended 14% below the no-shock path because essentially every reviewing firm froze pay for three and a half years. A firm that plans to shrink faster than attrition gets nothing from pay, so it wants a cut and freezes. In the data raises slowed but continued (job-stayer nominal wage growth fell from about 4% to about 2% in 2009-11; Grigsby, Hurst and Yildirmaz 2021).

The channels tested and ruled out: no customer market (rho_c = 1; deflation smaller but a deep, persistent recession, u +5 points for years), learned share response, full-cost customer value, and a higher firm elasticity (sigma_f = 0.8).

Fix (default): Weiss (1980) adverse selection in hiring and quits, the user's JMP mechanism and Campbell and Kamlani's (1997) leading reason firms do not cut pay. Pay then buys worker quality even for a shrinking firm. Bust (4 seeds): u +2.5 points for about three years with slow recovery, output -3%, investment -7%, consumption -3%, price level -8% (data: core prices about -4% relative to trend in 2008-11). Long run (4 seeds): u 3.2-3.6% (s.d. 1.3-1.6), inflation 1.4-1.7%, growth 1.5-1.65%, wage share 0.47, K/Y 1.2-1.3, wealth/GDP 6.7, price changes 27-30% a quarter (below the 35-40% target; the menu cost needs recalibrating), measured price over marginal cost about 1.9 (measured at posted pay per head, not per unit of quality).

### Robustness of the Weiss result (16 seeds) and the skill distribution

16 seeds, 20% asset bust. Weiss on (default): u +2.5 points for about three years, then back to baseline. Output -2 to -3% for two to three years, price level -5 to -7%. Weiss off: u +2.5 points at the peak but gone within a year, then below baseline; output barely moves; price level -10.5%. The default stands.

Caveat (the user's JMP, Fire_Sale.tex, appendix A.4, following Weiss 1980): a true Weiss wage floor exists only if output per dollar, E[q | q <= q*] / q*, has an interior maximum. Under the lognormal skill distribution (the default, s.d. 0.5) this ratio falls monotonically in q* at every dispersion, so there is no floor. Under lognormal, quality enters pay setting as a reason pay is worth something to a shrinking firm, not as a floor. With a mass at low skill (rectified normal, z_min 0.05) the ratio peaks at about half of workers, so a floor exists. On 4 seeds that version gives a smaller recession and more deflation; it is not yet calibrated (the floor binds in normal times).

### Minimum wage (option `min_wage`, off)

Policy rule: earnings per worker at least W_min, revised every 4 quarters to kaitz x published median earnings of the employed (kaitz 0.45; across 30 states the Kaitz index runs from 0.37 to 0.59, the federal minimum alone is 0.28, Dube 2025). Pooled firms pay at least W_min per head. Output-paying firms lay off and do not hire workers whose output (z w) is worth less than W_min, so those workers can only go to pooled firms (quality beliefs account for this).

Finding: it does not bind at pooled firms (0% of employed within 5% of the minimum), and it removes the output-paying option for 13% of workers (30% at kaitz 0.6). Unemployment is unchanged within noise. The reason is structural: 74% of workers are at pooled firms, which all pay about the same per head, so the pooled wage is the median. Earnings p10/p50 is 0.99 (data, hourly: about 0.5) and p90/p50 is 1.45 (data: about 2). The model has no low-wage jobs for a minimum to bind on.

### Pooled pay as the bottom of the wage distribution (service jobs), in progress

Idea (user): pooled, posted, per-head pay belongs to low-wage service jobs (flat posted rates, bunching at the minimum: Cengiz et al. 2019; posting more common for less-educated workers: Hall and Krueger 2012). Pay by measured output belongs to the rest.
- Pooled firms' pay review measured turnover against the overall going wage (quit elasticity 8), so pooled pay stayed at about 1.03 x the market average even though cost per unit of quality is lowest at the lowest pay (lognormal). New options: `x_lo`/`x_hi` (pay range considered) and `pool_own_market` (pooled firms benchmark against pooled firms, as output-paying firms do among themselves).
- With pooled firms the majority (15 of 20), the pooled wage is the median by construction. With 14-15 output-paying firms, pooled pay falls to 0.75 x median and earnings p10/p50 = 0.6, p90/p50 = 1.9 (data: about 0.5 and 2). But the pooled firms shrink to 3-5% of employment: they sell the same good as output-paying firms at a higher cost per unit of quality and lose customers. In the data, low-wage services are a separate product market. Next: a service good with its own expenditure share, so that demand pins the size of the pooled sector.

### Two sectors (option `services`, off) and the value of a worker's output (default fix)

- **Fix (default, `mrp_stock`):** when deciding whom to keep and hire, a firm valued a worker's output as if it had no stock on hand. A firm with many quarters of inventory therefore never laid anyone off. A worker's output is now valued as the newsvendor marginal unit: sold at marginal revenue if demand exceeds the stock (output plus expected carry-over), otherwise carried forward at the inventory value. Stored goods keep their value, so goods firms hoard labor; perishable output does not. Default economy (4 seeds): u 3.1-3.2% (s.d. 1.1-1.3), inflation 1.3-1.5%, growth about 1.55%, unchanged otherwise.
- **Sector price indexes:** each firm benchmarks its price against its own sector's published index (CPI goods and services are published separately); capital is valued at the overall index. Identical to before with one sector.
- **Service sector (in progress):** households spend a share s_sv (0.25) of consumption on services, produced by the pooled-pay firms; investment buys goods only; customer markets and spillovers operate within sectors; services perish (inv_decay_sv = 0.9). With 14 goods firms and 6 service firms, service employment settles near 25% (the intended share). The economy is not yet stable: service firms produce to stock a quarter ahead (as goods firms do), stock out, learn from censored sales, and shrink. Next: services produced to order within the quarter (staffing as capacity, unused capacity lost), the way restaurants and shops work.

### Labor flows and the policy rule (defaults changed, Nov 23)

- **Separation rate 0.055 per quarter (was 0.03).** Measured flows into unemployment were 1.4% a quarter, because most separated workers find a job within the quarter. JOLTS layoffs run about 1.2% a month, and some quits lead to unemployment. Unemployment was 2.7%, below the US normal of about 4-4.5% (CBO natural rate about 4.4%). Now u is about 4.7%.
- **Policy rule.** Smoothing 0.85 (was 0.95; Clarida, Gali and Gertler 2000 estimate about 0.8). Unemployment-gap weight 0.25 per quarter (was 0.1; Taylor 1993's 0.5 on the output gap is about 1 on the unemployment gap by Okun's law). With u* = 5% and the old low natural rate, the central bank saw permanent overheating: inflation fell to 0.4%. With the higher separation rate, inflation is 1.8%.
- **20% asset bust (8 seeds, new defaults):** u +1.7 points at quarter 4 and +3.8 at quarter 8, back to baseline by quarter 24. Policy rate cut 2.5-2.7 points. Price level -5% at quarter 4 and -8% at quarter 16. Output -1 to -1.5%.

### Two-sector bug fixes (services option)

- Investment, firm net worth and loans were priced with the sum of within-sector share-weighted prices, about twice the price level with two sectors, so investment ran at a third of output. Machines are now priced with the goods index, and households use the Cobb-Douglas consumption index.
- Services are produced to order within the quarter (capacity is the workforce; unused capacity is lost). The stock cap for service firms is the expected output of their workforce, not inventory.
- A customer lost to a stock-out is valued at the normal margin (the long-run Lerner margin), not today's margin. Otherwise pricing below cost made lost customers look worthless, and service firms that could not produce priced below cost and stayed there.
- Pooled firms benchmark pay against their workers' outside option (`pool_outside`): observed output per head x the going wage at output-paying firms. Benchmarking against the overall average put pooled pay at the median; benchmarking against other pooled firms made it ratchet up (to 1.5x the median), since each firm wanting to grow pays above its sector's average.
- With services (14 goods firms, 6 service firms, services 25% of consumption, `pool_outside`), 2 seeds: u 4.4% (s.d. 0.6-0.8), inflation 1.5-1.7%, service employment 32%, pooled pay 0.82 x median, earnings p10/p50 0.80 and p90/p50 2.0 (data about 0.5 and 2), price over marginal cost 1.58, wage share 0.45.

### New defaults (Nov 23): two sectors, participation, neutral-rate learning, menu cost

- **Services on by default** (14 goods firms paying by output, 6 service firms paying per head; services 25% of household spending; pooled pay benchmarked on the workers' outside option).
- **Participation (`olf`).** A working-age person without a job searches if UI (paid only to searchers, for 2 quarters after a job loss) plus the published job-finding rate x a quarter's earnings covers a personal search cost, kappa x earnings. kappa is persistent per person: lognormal, median 0.1, log s.d. 1. Measured unemployment counts searchers only. Result: labor-force participation about 96.5% of working-age people, unemployment 4.4-4.6% (2 seeds, no services). Re-entry and dropping out move with the published job-finding rate.
- **Neutral-rate learning (`rstar_learn`).** The central bank revises its neutral-rate estimate by 0.01 x the inflation miss each quarter. With a fixed 5% neutral rate, inflation ran at 1.4-1.5%. Now inflation is 2.1-2.2%.
- **Menu cost 0.03** (was 0.035): price changes 32% of quarters at 0.035, 43% at 0.025; target 35-40%.
- **Firm-specific efficiency (`firm_eff`, off).** With 20 firms, idiosyncratic efficiency shocks do not wash out: at a stationary s.d. of 0.25 the economy breaks (u above 60%); at 0.1, unemployment volatility rises about fourfold. Firm wages still do not track efficiency (correlation about -0.1). Realistic firm heterogeneity needs many more firms per market and a pay rule in which more productive firms want to be bigger (Burdett-Mortensen).

### Household credit lines (`cred_lim`, default 1.5) and a credit-standards shock

- **Design.** The buffer may go to -cred_lim x reference income (credit cards, HELOCs) at the bank loan rate + 0.02 a quarter. The buffer is valued as v(x + eps_B + cred_lim/beta), so cash and unused credit are both liquidity. Borrowing creates deposits (Werner). Debt beyond the line (after income falls) is charged off: the household's balance is reset to the line and the bank's equity falls by the same amount, so reserves stay at zero. The no-invasion branch of the mental-accounts problem had let debt roll and compound; the line now always binds.
- **Calibration.** At a 1.5-quarter line, household debt is 0.13 of quarterly GDP and 34% of people are in debt (data: revolving credit about 0.19 of quarterly GDP; about a third of households carry card debt). Quarterly charge-offs are about 1.2% of balances (about 4.8% a year; card charge-offs run about 3-4%).
- **On by default (cred_lim = 1.5).** An earlier check that showed deep slumps measured quarters 150-400 and so included the start-up transient (households start with no debt). Measured over quarters 300-600 (2 seeds): u 4.3-4.6% (s.d. 0.8-1.4), inflation 2.5-2.7%, growth about 1.8%, price over marginal cost 1.58, wage share 0.46, wealth/GDP 6.4. Without credit: u 4.3-4.4%, inflation 1.9%.
- **Credit-standards shock** (`SHOCK=credit` in `bust_diag.py`): banks cut lines by half, decaying at 0.95 a quarter. On impact consumption falls 5.5% and output 4.7%, as borrowers must repay at once. Unemployment is +1.9 points at a year and recovery takes about two years. A gradual phase-in of the cut would be more realistic.

### Utilization revisited (Nov 23) and labor hoarding (option `hoard`, off)

Under the current defaults, utilization blew up within 30 quarters: with flat marginal cost the goods sector produced in bursts (employment from 2,000 to 24 heads). A firm holding stock valued a worker's output at the inventory value, just below the wage, and laid everyone off until the stock ran down; layoffs cost nothing. `hoard` keeps a worker while MRP covers the wage net of the expected cost of rehiring later (share of the wage; hiring costs about half a quarter's wage, Blatter et al.). With hoard = 0.3 and eps = 6, utilization is stable on 2 seeds (quarters 250-500): u 5.6-6.3% (s.d. about 2), inflation 2.1-2.3%, but capacity utilization 0.90-0.92 (planned 0.8) and K/Y up to 1.66. eps = 7.7 still blows up after about 370 quarters. Without utilization, hoarding at 0.3 destabilizes the default economy (u 8.2%, s.d. 7), so it stays off.

### Experiments with the Nov 23 defaults (8 seeds) and the bust decomposition

- **Policy-rate shock (+1 point, decaying 0.8 a quarter).** Consumption no longer rises on impact (+0.01%, was +0.3% when every household was a net saver), since borrowers offset savers. Output moves only -0.05 to -0.1%, inflation -0.1 to -0.3 points: monetary transmission is weak. The interest-sensitive spending of the real economy (housing, durables) is missing.
- **Stimulus check (1% of annual GDP):** consumption +0.3% on impact; 3.45% of quarterly GDP spent over 8 quarters out of a 4% transfer. **Withholding cut:** 3.35%.
- **Credit-standards shock, phased in over about a year (50% cut in lines, decaying 0.95):** consumption -1.1% on impact, u +0.8 points for about two years, price level -5%. It is modest because revolving debt is about 0.13 of quarterly GDP; a 2008-size credit shock needs mortgages and housing.
- **Bust decomposition (`price_decomp.py`, 4 seeds).** In the first year prices fall with the demand belief (published market size about -4%). After that prices track unit cost, which tracks posted pay. Pay ends 10% below the no-shock path after 5.5 years (about 1.8 points a year slower growth, in line with 2009-14 wage data). Prices follow pay one for one: the markup does not rise, whereas in 2008-11 core prices fell only about 4% relative to trend (financially constrained firms raised markups, Gilchrist et al. 2017). Next: an external finance premium that rises as firms' cash flow weakens, which raises the discount rate on customer investment and so markups in downturns.

### External finance premium (`efp`, default on, Nov 23)

The bank prices each firm's loan for its expected loss: PD = pd0 (cov_ref / coverage)^pd_el, with pd0 = 0.004 a quarter, cov_ref = 4, pd_el = 2, loss given default 0.5. Rate = (1 + rL)/(1 - PD x LGD) - 1. Coverage = smoothed operating cash flow / interest, about 4.2 in the long run, giving premia of about 0.75 points a year. The firm's cost of funds (r_own) is its own rate, so a firm with weak cash flow discounts customer investment, capital and hiring more heavily (Bernanke-Gertler; Gilchrist et al. 2017). Long run unchanged (u 4.5-4.7%, inflation 2.5-2.9%). 20% asset bust (16 seeds): u +0.8 points at quarter 4, +2.4 at quarter 8, back to baseline by quarter 16; output -1.3% at its trough; price level -8%, recovering to -3 to -5% after six years (was -8 to -10% without the premium).

### Consumer durables with installment credit (`durables`, off)

- **Design.** Each household holds a durable stock (cars, appliances) depreciating 5% a quarter, with a target of 2 quarters of income at goods prices. (S,s) rule: upgrade when psi x gap share >= (installment rate + depreciation) x theta x gap share + a fixed cost (Grossman-Laroque), so higher rates widen the band. Purchases are goods demand. They are financed with 20-quarter amortizing installment loans at the loan rate + 0.005, with 10% down, if total installment payments stay within 15% of income (lenders' DTI rule). A new loan is consolidated with any old balance. The loan creates the deposit that pays the seller. The last payment clears the balance; balances of the deceased are charged off. Reserves stay at zero.
- **Results.** A first version financed purchases on the card line: half the households bought at quarter 0 and nearly all hit their limits within a year, giving a deleveraging depression at the ZLB. With installment loans and initial stocks spread over the band, the start-up depression lasts about 250 quarters. Then (seed 2, quarters 400-500): u 4.0% (s.d. 0.4), inflation 2.3%, durable purchases 6.9% of consumption (data: 10-12% of PCE), installment debt 0.34 of quarterly GDP (data about 0.23), 92% of people with a loan (data about a third with auto loans). Policy-rate shock (+1 point, 8 seeds): output -0.4 to -0.6% in the first three quarters, against about -0.05% without durables.
- **Recalibrated.** Households pay cash out of the buffer above its target and finance the rest (10% down on the financed part). Fixed cost 0.064 (replacement about every 16 quarters), target stock 3 quarters of income, psi 0.34. Seed 2, quarters 400-500: u 4.2% (s.d. 0.7), inflation 2.4%, durable purchases 7.2% of consumption, installment debt 0.18 of quarterly GDP (data about 0.23), 76% with a loan (data about a third). The start-up depression still lasts about 250 quarters.

### 2008 scenario and scarring (Nov 23)

- **2008 scenario** (`SHOCK=2008`: 20% asset bust plus a phased-in halving of household credit lines; 16 seeds): u +1.9 points at quarter 4, +4.65 at quarter 8, +2.3 at quarter 12, back to baseline by quarter 16. Output -3.0% at its trough, investment -9.5%, policy rate cut 4.8 points, price level -12% (data: unemployment +5 points, core prices about -4% relative to trend). The peak is right; recovery is about two years against about six in the data, and deflation is about three times too large. Both shocks decay at 0.95 a quarter by construction.
- **Scarring (option `scar`, off):** skill decays 1% a quarter after two quarters without a job and recovers 2% a quarter on the job, up to its original level (Davis and von Wachter 2011). In the 2008 scenario (8 seeds) u peaks at +5.0 and output at -3.7%, with the same recovery speed.
- Measured unemployment weights people by skill (efficiency units); headcount gives 6.1% against 5.9% (correlation 0.99).
- **Final default calibration (4 seeds, quarters 300-600):** u 4.5-4.9% (s.d. 0.9-1.6), inflation 2.5-2.9%, growth 1.8-2.0%, price over marginal cost 1.6, wage share 0.43-0.46, K/Y 1.15-1.25, wealth/GDP 6.5, prices change 35-38% of quarters.

### Where the excess deflation comes from (Nov 23, end of session)

- 2008 scenario under flat marginal cost (utilization, hoard = 0.3, eps = 6; 8 seeds): the recovery becomes slow, as after 2008 (u stays 1-2 points high for about ten years, output 3-4% below for several years). But deflation is no smaller: the price level falls 11% within a year and the real wage jumps 11%. So steep marginal cost is not the source. With spare capacity, each firm cuts its price to win customers from rivals who all do the same: the customer-market price war, now in a slump. Blinder et al. (1998) rank coordination failure among the top reasons firms do not cut prices.
- The candidate fix is `share_learn` (firms learn how much share a relative price cut actually wins). With the current defaults it makes the start-up much worse (u 35-43% for over 100 quarters): its moments start at zero, so the learned slope swings early. It needs moments initialized at the prior. Then test the 2008 scenario with utilization + share_learn: the target is slow recovery with core-like disinflation.

### Firm heterogeneity (in progress; user: real firms differ in productivity, and the number of firms is not the problem)

- The earlier note ("with 20 firms, idiosyncratic shocks become aggregate") was wrong as a reason. A 2% aggregate productivity wobble cannot produce 60% unemployment; mechanisms inside the model amplified productivity differences.
- **Zombies.** Default was only a write-down: the bank cut the loan back to zero net worth and the firm continued with the same low productivity. Low-efficiency firms were written down every quarter, and the external finance premium pushed their loan rates toward 15% a quarter.
- **Write-downs are bookkeeping noise.** Firms finance capital entirely with loans and pay out all accounting profit, so book net worth hovers at zero. Even without firm efficiency, all firms get small write-downs most quarters late in a run. Write-downs are the wrong exit signal.
- **Exit and entry (added, active with `firm_eff`).** A firm whose smoothed earnings (last year) stay negative for 4 quarters exits (no going-concern value; Hopenhayn 1992). An entrant takes its slot and assets (a bankruptcy sale), with a new efficiency draw from the stationary distribution, a fresh credit assessment, and half its customers.
- **Results (seed 1, 500 quarters).** sd_a = 0.15: after the start-up, u 6-12%, 0.1-0.3 exits a quarter, firm size positively related to efficiency. sd_a = 0.25: u swings between 4% and 79%, 2-5 exits a quarter, and the s.d. of log firm employment reaches 1-2 (winner-take-all), with size unrelated to efficiency.
- **Why.** With a demand elasticity of about 8, a 0.25 cost gap means shares differing by a factor of about 50. Capital adjusts slowly, so a firm that becomes efficient cannot serve the customers its price attracts. In the data, size dispersion is driven mostly by persistent demand differences (Foster, Haltiwanger and Syverson 2008: high-TFPQ firms charge lower prices, demand shifters explain more of size); revenue-productivity dispersion is much smaller than physical (Hsieh and Klenow 2009).
- **Next.** Two persistent firm traits, efficiency (cost) and appeal (demand), calibrated jointly to TFPQ dispersion, the firm size distribution and the price-productivity correlation.
- **Appeal (added).** Each firm has a persistent log demand shifter q (quality, reputation) in buyers' logit choices, AR(1) with the efficiency persistence; entrants draw a new one.
- **The real culprit: the demand belief lagged the customer base.** Firms learned their market position slowly from noisy sales (constant gain), while their customer base can collapse within a few quarters. A high-appeal firm's belief ended up 20-270 times its actual demand: it overstocked, overhired and overpriced, lost its customers, corrected, and briefly won 39% of the market, in a cycle. In a customer market a firm knows its customer base (its customer records). `cust_belief` (default) sets the market position to the customer base's share of total final spending.
- **Results.** With identical firms: u 4.3-4.4% (s.d. 0.6-0.8), inflation 2.4%, calmer than before. With heterogeneous firms (sd_a = 0.15, sd_q = 0.5): u 5.8-6.2% (s.d. about 2), firm size related to efficiency (correlation 0.45-0.77) and appeal, more efficient firms mostly charge lower prices, exits about 0.25 a quarter (about 5% of firms a year; data about 8-10% for establishments). At sd_a = 0.25 (TFPQ dispersion in Foster, Haltiwanger and Syverson) with sd_q = 0.5: stable, u 5-13%, but exits about 16% a year. sd_q = 1.0 breaks down.
- **New defaults:** firm_eff on (sd_a = 0.15, sd_q = 0.5), cust_belief on. Idiosyncratic shocks raise the frequency of price changes (Golosov-Lucas): 63-69% a quarter at menu cost 0.03, 57% at 0.05, 48% at 0.08 (where stock-outs rise, fill 0.83). Menu cost 0.05. With heterogeneity, reallocation creates layoffs inside the model, so the exogenous separation rate is lowered to 0.045; u stays about 5.8%.
- **Remaining gaps:** productivity dispersion below the data (0.15 vs about 0.26), price-change frequency above target, exit rate below data at 0.15 and above at 0.25, u about 1.5 points high.

### Federal Reserve, Treasury account and government purchases (Nov 23; user: the government's account is at the Fed; make the Fed realistic: OMOs, IOR, DR, RR = 0)

- **Before.** The government balance was booked as a deposit at the commercial bank. Numerically that equals a TGA at the Fed with deficits financed by bills sold to the bank, and with the Fed lending reserves back to the bank on demand when the TGA is positive. But the bills' rate on a positive balance (rb) and the capital charge on bills were wrong. With the deficit rule off, the government ran surpluses (net creditor of about 43% of GDP).
- **Fed (floor system).** The Taylor rule sets the target. Interest on reserves = target. Discount rate = target + 0.25 points a year. No reserve requirement. Balance sheet: assets are bills bought in open-market operations (SOMA) plus discount loans; liabilities are reserves plus the TGA. Each quarter OMOs set SOMA so that reserves = 10% of annual GDP (US 2024: about 11%) given the TGA; the discount window covers any shortfall. Fed profit (bill interest + discount-loan interest - IOR) is remitted to the Treasury.
- **Treasury.** Keeps the TGA at 2.5% of annual GDP and issues bills (paying the target rate) for the rest of its net position. The bank holds the bills the Fed does not, as primary dealer. Treasuries carry a zero risk weight in the bank's capital rule.
- **Government purchases.** 17% of trend nominal GDP, half goods and half services. The deficit rule is on: the tax allows the deficit that keeps debt at its target (100% of annual GDP) as nominal GDP grows.
- **Check.** "Reserves are zero" is replaced by: the bank's reserves implied by its balance sheet equal the reserves on the Fed's balance sheet (difference about 1e-16).
- **Results (seed 1, quarters 300-500).** Bills 1.12 x annual GDP (p5 0.52, p95 1.67); Fed holdings 0.12; reserves 0.10; no discount-window lending; payroll tax 46% of wages (the only tax carries all revenue); u 6.1%; inflation 1.8%.
- **Next (user: banks as primary dealers in Treasury auctions).** Explicit auction: the bank bids a yield at IOR plus a small dealer margin (its alternative is reserves at IOR). Distribution: the retirement fund buys bills for the safe part of its portfolio. OMOs stay secondary-market trades with the bank.
- **Auctions and distribution (added).** The Treasury auctions its bills each quarter. The bank, as primary dealer, bids the yield at IOR + dealer_margin (0 by default), since its alternative is reserves at IOR; the auction clears there and bills pay that yield. The retirement fund buys bills from the dealer for the safe part of its portfolio (15% of its value, standing in for all non-bank holders: pension and money funds, foreigners) and sells them back when the Fed's OMOs need them. Interest: the government pays the bill yield on all bills; the Fed remits its profit; bank, fund and Treasury flows net to zero. Seed 1, quarters 300-500: bills 0.98 x annual GDP, held by the Fed 0.12, the fund 0.79, the bank 0.07 (US: Fed about 0.2 of debt held by the public, banks about 0.06); reserves 0.10; u 5.5%; inflation 1.4%; payroll tax 43%. Balance-sheet check passes.

### Cash dropped; several competing banks (Nov 23)

- **Cash.** Dropped deliberately. In a floor system currency only changes the size of OMOs; seigniorage is about 0.1-0.2% of GDP. Modern bank runs (SVB, Signature, First Republic in 2023) are runs of uninsured deposits to other banks and money funds, not into cash, so runs stay studiable without cash.
- **Banks (user: several banks with competition; `multi_bank`, default on, NB = 5).** Each household and firm banks with one bank: deposits, credit line and installment loans, or business loans are there. Each bank posts its own deposit and loan rates as a best response to its rivals' last posted rates, given its own depositors (Baumol-Tobin savings) and its own loss history. Firms pay their bank's rate plus their external finance premium. Each quarter 2% of households and 5% of firms re-shop and choose a bank by logit on posted rates (the same logit the banks use), moving their balances; the payment settles in reserves. Each bank has its own income statement (its customers' interest, losses and charge-offs; its share of bill, reserve and interbank interest by its positions; its share of the fund's deposit interest), its own equity and dividends (above 8% of its business loans). Banks short of reserves borrow from banks with a surplus at IOR. A bank with negative equity is recapitalized by its owner, the fund (failure, deposit insurance and runs come next). Checks: bank reserve positions sum to the Fed's reserves (about 1e-14); balance-sheet check passes.
- **Results (2 seeds, quarters 300-600):** u 5.4-5.6% (s.d. 2.0), inflation 1.9-2.1%, growth 1.9-2.3%, price over marginal cost 1.63, wage share 0.45, K/Y 1.0, wealth/GDP 6.7. Posted rates differ across banks by a few basis points; market shares move with switching. Recapitalizations are large only in the start-up slump.

### Who absorbs new debt: the Fed, endogenously (user: in 2020 the Fed bought to absorb the excess and keep long rates stable; QE is endogenous)

- Replaced the reserve target with private demand at the going yield plus a Fed that absorbs the rest. The fund holds 10% of its portfolio in Treasuries (about 0.7 of GDP, standing in for non-bank holders). Banks hold 5% of their deposits as Treasury liquidity. The Fed buys whatever supply exceeds that, so yields stay put however much debt is issued, and reserves are the result (2020: Fed Treasury holdings rose about $2.4T and reserves from about $1.7T to over $3T). A first version with a QE rule tied to the policy rate's lower bound was wrong and was reverted.
- **Test** (seed 3, from quarter 400, extra spending of 10% of GDP for 4 quarters): Fed holdings 0.08 -> 0.23 of GDP, reserves 0.06 -> 0.21; fund and bank holdings flat or falling. But the economy starts at full employment, so the deficit mainly raises inflation (policy rate to 13%) and debt/GDP barely rises. 2020's deficit came with collapsing output.
- **Caveats.** With bills only, "stable yields" means the bill yield, which is pinned at IOR. The full version needs long-term bonds with private demand for duration (preferred habitat, Vayanos-Vila) and the Fed absorbing supply to hold the term premium steady. Debt/GDP wanders widely in normal times (the fiscal rule responds weakly, 0.02 of the gap).


### Long Treasury bonds, the term premium and the Fed's balance-sheet rule (Nov 23; user: the Fed can decide to lower or raise long-term rates)

- **Bond.** A perpetuity whose coupon decays at 0.975 a quarter (Woodford), so one price covers the long end (duration about 28 quarters). Price q = 1/(1 + y - 0.975). Holdings are in coupon units; each quarter the coupons are paid and holdings decay.
- **Treasury.** Keeps 70% of its marketable debt (market value) in long bonds and issues bills for the rest; it does not buy bonds back. Its net position is kept at market value, so revaluations of long debt change it. The tax rule sees interest as the coupon less the part that repays principal.
- **Long yield.** The expected average short rate over the bond's duration (34% weight on today's bill rate, 66% on the Fed's published neutral-rate estimate) plus the term premium: TP = 1% a year + 5 points a year per unit of GDP of long debt private holders carry above 0.5 of GDP (preferred habitat, Vayanos-Vila 2021; QE event studies give about 0.5 points per 10% of GDP).
- **Fed rule (a policy primitive).** Its term-premium target stays at normal against supply shocks: it buys whatever private holders will not take at the target (2020). When the short-rate rule asks for a rate below zero, the target is cut by 0.25 x the shortfall (at most 1.6 points a year; QE1-QE3 lowered the 10-year premium by about 1.5 points). Otherwise the target returns to normal at 10% a quarter. The Fed never sells: it shrinks by runoff (2017-19, 2022-), and while it holds more than the target needs, the market sets a premium below target. The long yield is never pushed below zero.
- **Holders.** The fund holds 90% of private long bonds and banks 10% (split by deposits), at market value: coupons are income and price changes hit fund NAV and bank equity (duration risk, SVB 2023). Trades with the Fed are at the market price.
- **Fed accounts.** Bonds at amortized cost for income; market value for equity. Losses (user, Nov 23: use the deferred asset): by default the Fed carries them as a deferred asset, as it has since 2011. A loss is paid in new reserves, and remittances to the Treasury resume only once later income has paid the asset down. Treasury indemnity (Bank of England) is the option `fed_indemnity=True`. There a loss is paid in newly created reserves: the IOR the Fed cannot cover from income is credited to banks, so reserves (and, through bank income and dividends, deposits) grow. Without currency (a zero-interest liability whose seigniorage pays the deferred asset down) the Fed's negative equity is reserves paying the IOR with no assets behind them, so it compounds at the IOR. With the policy rate above nominal growth it explodes relative to GDP: with runoff-only QT, the deferred asset reached 1.0-1.4 of GDP and reserves 1.1-1.7 of GDP by quarter 550 (2 seeds), with no visible inflation, because reserves paying the IOR are as good as bills paying the same rate. Indemnity and the deferred asset are the same for the consolidated government (Treasury bills vs reserves at the same rate); they differ in who issues the liability and in what the fiscal rule sees (it sees Treasury debt, not the Fed's negative equity).
- **Transmission.** The term premium's deviation from normal shifts the fund's discount rate (portfolio balance) and the firms' long-run cost of funds.
- **Checks.** Balance-sheet check 8e-16. Banks' reserve positions sum to the Fed's reserves (1e-15; discount-window loans now enter the borrowing banks' reserves, which the old check missed when the window was used). Determinism passes.
- **Calibration** (600 quarters, burn-in 300; seed 1 / seed 2; old model without long bonds in brackets): u 5.0 / 5.4 [5.3 / 5.5]; inflation 2.0 / 2.0 [1.6 / 1.8]; policy rate 6.2 / 6.3 [4.9]; growth 2.0; debt/GDP 0.70 / 0.67 [0.86]; Fed long holdings 0.21 of GDP (US before 2020: about 0.15-0.2); mean term premium 0.0 / -0.1 points. The premium is below normal on average because the Fed runs off earlier QE slowly (US ACM 10-year premium about -0.5 to 0 in 2016-20).
- **Tests** (`fed_test.py`, 2 seeds).
  - Deficit of 15% of quarterly GDP for 4 quarters: the premium rises about 0.3-0.6 points under both rules, because the Fed starts above its target-consistent holdings and lets the market take the supply.
  - 2008 scenario: the Fed buys about 8% of GDP of long bonds (passive Fed: 3%); reserves rise 6% of GDP; the premium stays flat (passive: +0.3 points); the long yield falls 2.6 points (2.3); unemployment recovers somewhat faster after quarter 12.
- **Open.**
  - The policy rate is volatile (sd about 3 points) and often at zero, so the Fed carries legacy QE much of the time.
  - Debt/GDP still wanders (sd 0.2-0.3).
  - Long bonds raise the average policy rate by about 1.3 points (neutral-rate learning reacts to slightly higher inflation).
- **Fed income** (seed 1, 200 quarters after burn-in, indemnity run). Remittances average 0.05% of GDP a year and are negative in a third of quarters (US before 2022: about 0.4-0.5%). Earnings on the assets backing the Treasury's account (a liability paying no interest) are 0.16% of GDP. The carry on long bonds (long yield minus bill rate, times holdings) is 0.06%. Bills pay exactly the IOR, so they earn the Fed nothing net. The carry is small because the term premium averages zero, and the Fed buys long bonds when the short rate is low and holds them when it rises. So the deferred asset grows (0.3-0.5 of GDP by quarter 400 in the earlier runs).
- **QE only at the lower bound** (Nov 23). The term-premium cut used to start as soon as the short-rate rule turned negative. Because of smoothing, that happened in 13-21% of quarters while the rate was still positive. Now the cut needs the rate at its lower bound (at zero 1-7% of quarters).
- **Deferred asset diagnostics** (quarters 300-700, 2 seeds; user agreed the asymmetry is realistic: early income goes to the Treasury, later interest shortfalls stay with the Fed, and the Fed realizes no losses because it only runs off).
  - The Fed never remits after burn-in, and the deferred asset grows to 1.2-7 times GDP.
  - The term premium is below the Fed's target in 85-90% of quarters, and Fed long holdings average 0.18-0.19 of GDP. This is not QE. It is a ratchet: the Fed buys whenever debt growth pushes the premium above target, and it cannot sell when debt/GDP falls back (it wanders 0.3-1.2). So the premium averages about 0 and the Fed earns almost no carry.
  - Once its equity is below minus the TGA, the IOR on the reserves with nothing behind them is a loss each quarter, so the deferred asset compounds at the IOR. The model's average policy rate (about 6%) exceeds nominal growth (about 4%), so it explodes relative to GDP. In the US, r < g most of the time, and currency gives the Fed income.
